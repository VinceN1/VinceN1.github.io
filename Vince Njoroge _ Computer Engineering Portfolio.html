Projects

NLP Course

AI Benchmarking

In my Natural Language Processing course, we were tasked with benchmarking several large language models to standards of our own choosing. This led us to investigate further the metrics that usually guide large language models, and papers that were attempting to determine a closer version of the intelligence or smartness of models to basic human reasoning and thinking. And so, while we would draw from Hugging Face sets of commonly used question sets in mathematical and Boolean logic devised by Google, we would also pair them with counterfactual and negative qualifiers, to truly test the reasoning of these programs. In addition to the questions posed, we would also test for the influence of context or chain-of-thought reasoning versus single-shot questions. Overall, my team was able to deliver an interesting picture concerning LLM accuracy. One in which ChatGPT 5.5 performed better according to simple positive affirmation and mathematical performance, but one in which a significantly less developed, smaller-parameter model, Llama 3, did better according to logical invariants and counterfactual questions.

Team of three

Supervised Fine-Tuning

Given three datasets over three disparate domains, as a group consisting of myself alongside two of my classmates, we set out to explore the effect of training under methods such as SFT, LoRA, and QLoRA, and whether such differences should hold in different areas. The domains for the datasets we used were financial, legal, and medical, and our justification for them was not only their prevalence and importance in the modern world, and thus the necessity to explore ways in which one can help train models to better grasp their contents, but also their larger differences in content format, for instance multiple choice questions with medical, open-ended answers with financial, and a mix with legal. Thus, we were not only training our models under these domains to see differences because of method but also because of content type, and so there were two main types of comparisons to draw: between methods, and within them.

Operating Systems Course

Operating Systems Project

In my operating systems course, I had the opportunity to work on a project of choice, and thus devised implementing, alongside a classmate, an x86 bootloader. To the unfamiliar, a bootloader is responsible for the boot-up sequence of a computer, in which the operating system is loaded as the different components within the computer turn on. In the unfolding of the project, I was able to navigate the allocation of memory used in the sequence, the file system, and boot sequence failures.
